Today's big story
Ethereum’s exit queue just hit its longest wait of the year, and almost none of it is about price.
On September 30 MetaMask disclosed that part of its staking infrastructure had been compromised, and began a precautionary mass exit of its validators. The on-chain researcher Kaden found that fee rewards from 18 of the 19 MetaMask validators that proposed blocks inside the incident window had been redirected to an address funded through Tornado Cash. The breach itself is bounded, and the company’s own exit is expected to finish around October 7. The queue it created is not bounded in the same way. CoinDesk’s analysis of the exit data puts roughly 166,000 ETH waiting to leave the staking system on September 29 and roughly 851,000 ETH by October 2 — about 2% of the 43.6 million ETH staked, and well past the roughly 476,000 ETH that queued during May’s surge. MetaMask accounts for about 523,000 ETH of that, a little over two thirds. The estimated wait to get out reached 14.77 days, the longest since December 2025, and the queue to start staking has stretched to around 25 days with nearly 1.5 million ETH in line. The part worth keeping is this: not one of those validators is waiting because of a market. They are waiting because a withdrawal is a queue with a throughput limit, and one large operator leaving at once is enough to set the wait for everybody else. Every incident has two durations — how long the compromise lasted, and how long it takes everyone who lost confidence to act on it. The second is usually the expensive one, and it shows up in retention rather than in an incident report.

01 — Fintech pulse
A fortnight ago custody was a gap in a rule. This week it is a proposal, three charters and a lawsuit.
On October 1 the SEC proposed the custody framework it had been signalling, and its shape matters more than its arrival: two paths, not one. Registered advisers, registered investment companies and business development companies could rely on state trust companies as custodians, subject to checks on a company’s authority and its safeguards — and, where no permitted custodian is available, could hold the assets themselves under conditions. Chair Paul Atkins said on October 3 that the Commission had “proposed to close a gap that has left investment advisers and funds guessing how to effect lawful custody,” adding that “our work is not finished. More regulatory proposals are on the horizon.” Comments run 60 days from Federal Register publication. The adjacent fights moved too: the OCC’s September 18 conditional national trust charters to Bastion Platforms, Catena Trust Bank and Agora National Trust Bank are now being litigated, after the Independent Community Bankers of America sued the agency on October 2 in the District of Columbia under the Administrative Procedure Act to overturn the March 2 final rule those charters rest on — chief executive Rebeca Romero Rainey arguing that “Congress did not create the national trust charter as a side door into the banking system.” Outside the US the same subject is being settled with dates rather than briefs: South Korea’s rules letting stocks, bonds and funds move on-chain from February 2027, an EU proposal to widen MiCA’s reach, and the UK opening its authorisation window. And as a gauge of how quickly the mood is shifting, the SEC also cleared a Cboe BZX rule change allowing six Volatility Shares funds targeting three times the daily return on bitcoin and ether — in a market where the ceiling had been 2x.
Strip the venues away and every one of those documents argues about a single sentence: the conditions under which somebody may hold an asset that belongs to someone else. Segregation, consolidated supervision, insurance, the audit that proves it — each requirement exists to police the gap between your property and an institution’s balance sheet. Self-custody does not win that argument; it declines to enter it, because there is no commingling question where there is no custodian. What that leaves is the unglamorous half: whether the money path itself works. SwopPay checkout charges 0.5%, and the rate is the same on the card rail and the crypto rail — one number, not a headline with a quieter one behind it. Swop covers the network fee rather than passing it through; a cost absorbed, not a line item. Crypto and x402 payments are never identity-gated, so a seller is payable in USDC the moment they list a product — only the card rail requires merchant verification, because the card networks require it there. On that rail the proceeds never touch a Swop wallet at all: Stripe holds the fiat balance and pays the merchant’s bank. None of that is a feature anyone screenshots. It is just what has to be true before a payment is allowed to be boring.

02 — From the Journal
Sell digital products for crypto: how it works
Newest on the Swop Journal, and a useful counterweight to the custody fight above: selling a digital product for crypto means pricing it in a stablecoin, listing it somewhere a wallet can pay directly, and getting paid the moment the payment settles on-chain — no processor holding the funds, no payout schedule to wait out. The buyer signs from their own wallet instead of typing a card number, and increasingly the buyer is software: an AI agent paying over x402, with no checkout form to fill in at all. What changes versus a conventional digital-goods platform is custody and speed — the money lands in a wallet the seller controls, not in a merchant account somebody else is holding on their behalf.
The post also says the part most storefront pitches skip: settling the payment and delivering the file are two different problems. A platform can get the on-chain settlement exactly right and still leave the actual handover to a manual DM, so the question to ask before you run a real sale through anything is whether delivery fires automatically off the payment or depends on you remembering to follow up. That is the whole test for a money path — does it work when nobody is watching it. Also on the Journal: what is a wrapped token — a claim on a bridge, not on the original issuer.

03 — Day ahead
What to watch — the week’s first real datapoint lands this morning, two American League Game 2s tonight, and a set of minutes on Wednesday doing the heavy lifting.
📅 Macro Calendar
• Today, 10:00 ET — ISM services PMI for September, consensus looking for a small improvement to 55.7 from 55.4 in August. Read new orders, employment and prices paid rather than the headline; prices paid is where services inflation turns up last and leaves slowest
• Wednesday, 14:00 ET — minutes from the Federal Reserve’s September 15–16 meeting. With no CPI until October 14, the minutes are the week’s main event, and the question is how much disagreement the vote concealed
• The lens — traders spent the weekend pricing out an October hike, so the minutes will be read as confirmation or contradiction of a position the market has already taken. That is a different thing from the minutes settling it
• Also this week — an OPEC+ decision, Canadian employment on Friday, and ECB accounts. A light calendar overall, which tends to hand whatever does print more room to move the tape than it strictly deserves
⚖ Court Watch
• ICBA v. OCC, filed October 2 — the community bankers’ Administrative Procedure Act challenge in the US District Court for the District of Columbia, aimed at the OCC’s March 2 final rule tied to Interpretive Letter 1176 and the three conditional trust charters granted on September 18. A live complaint, not a ruling, and no hearing date yet
• Prediction markets, still the petition pool — the Ninth Circuit held on August 28 that sports event contracts are not swaps within the CFTC’s exclusive jurisdiction, declining to preempt Nevada’s gaming law and splitting with the Third Circuit. Robinhood petitioned the Supreme Court on September 9 and Crypto.com’s Nadex on September 11; Kalshi is separately seeking en banc rehearing. No cert grant, no argument date
• CFTC v. KalshiEX — a Rule 16 scheduling conference in the Massachusetts case is reported for October 13, and the agency’s prediction-market rulemaking sits at the White House for review. Rules landing while the circuits disagree can moot half the fight or sharpen it, and which one will be obvious from the text
• SEC custody proposal — the comment window runs 60 days from Federal Register publication. The comments worth reading will come from the firms that would rather be the qualified custodian than compete with people who hold their own keys
🎯 Tonight's Games · Swop Predictions vs. the Books
• Two American League Game 2s — White Sox at Guardians 5:00 ET and Yankees at Rays 8:00 ET, both on TBS, truTV and HBO Max. The National League series are not scheduled today
• Why Game 2 is the interesting price — Cleveland and New York are each reported to be trying to even their series, which is exactly what makes today’s quotes worth pulling up. In a five-game series the first result moves the series probability more than any single later game will, so Game 2 is the first clean look at what the market thinks one win was actually worth. A book reprices to balance its exposure; a prediction market reprices because the probability changed
• Check it rather than trust it — pull the live series prices in the app before first pitch and compare them to a sportsbook’s. The point of an order book is that you do not have to take our word for it
A book quotes a price and manages its exposure; a prediction market quotes the probability and reprices as the series moves. The divergence is the thing to look at.
