Today's big story
Bitget switches withdrawals back on today, four days after $387.5M walked out of its hot wallets.
On September 24 attackers drained roughly $387.5M from Bitget — the largest crypto hack of the year so far. CEO Gracy Chen’s first assessment put it at $351.6M; the number rose once Zcash and TRON balances left out of the initial count were added. What the attackers did not take is the detail worth sitting with: not private keys. Bitget says they compromised a backend system used to process wallet transactions and tricked its own internal approval flow into authorising the transfers, and it attributes the attack to suspected North Korean operators. User balances are being covered out of the $464M Bitget User Protection Fund, with withdrawals restarting in stages — Bitcoin from 08:00 UTC today, Ethereum tomorrow, Tether on the 30th, everything else by October 2. Bitget is offering 5% for freezing attacker funds and another 5% for recovery, but roughly $83M of the stolen XRP has already been moved somewhere Ripple’s freeze controls cannot reach. An insurance fund is a real protection and it is not the same protection as holding your own keys: one is a promise to make you whole, the other removes the need for the promise.

01 — Fintech pulse
The Fed just told stablecoin issuers what they may hold: short-term Treasuries, and not much else.
On September 24 the Federal Reserve proposed the rules that turn the GENIUS Act into operating requirements for payment stablecoin issuers: reserves held almost entirely in short-term Treasury bills or comparably liquid assets, standardised capital requirements, risk-management standards, and a new application process that board-supervised banks must clear before they may issue at all. It lands next to a Treasury proposal implementing the Act’s illicit-finance requirements, and after the CFTC moved to accept certain payment stablecoins as eligible collateral — Chairman Michael Selig framing tokenisation as a route to near-instantaneous settlement and real-time collateral mobility. The White House has finished reviewing how regulators will approve state-level licensing frameworks, so Treasury’s version of that could circulate shortly. Federal and state are not yet in step: on September 21 the Crypto Council for Innovation asked New York’s DFS to pause its own proposed issuer rule until the federal framework is final.
The through-line for anyone building on these rails is where the perimeter is being drawn: around issuers, reserves and service providers — not around the wallet holding the dollar. Swop’s position hasn’t moved and is worth restating plainly: crypto and x402 payments on Swop are never identity-gated, and a seller is payable in USDC the moment they list a product. Only the card rail requires merchant verification, because the card networks require it there. What changes as these rules land is not who has to identify themselves to us. It is which dollars are worth accepting — reserve composition stops being a footnote in a transparency report and becomes the thing that separates one stablecoin from another.

02 — From the Journal
What is a wrapped token? A claim on a bridge, not the issuer
Answer first: a wrapped token is a token minted on one chain to represent an asset that lives on another. A bridge or a custodian locks the original and issues a matching claim elsewhere, redeemable one-for-one in principle. It carries the same ticker and tracks the same price — what it does not carry is the same backing, and that gap is completely invisible in a wallet balance. Holding wBTC is not holding Bitcoin; it is holding one specific issuer’s promise to give you Bitcoin. The post’s practical test is three questions: who holds the lock, are the reserves independently verifiable, and what happens to your claim if that party fails. Today’s Bitget story is the same question asked of an exchange instead of a bridge.
