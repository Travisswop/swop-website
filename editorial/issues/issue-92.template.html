Today's big story
The SEC has proposed letting regulated funds hold their own keys — and the conditions it attached are a precise inventory of what custody actually costs.
On October 1 the SEC proposed rules giving investment advisers, investment companies and business development companies a defined way to hold crypto for clients. Chairman Paul Atkins framed it as “a clear regulatory framework for the custody of crypto assets, giving investment advisers and funds a compliant pathway where none existed before.” The headline is that self-custody is on the table at all. The detail is the fence around it: an adviser may hold a crypto asset itself only after establishing that no qualified custodian exists for that particular token, must reassess that quarterly, and must move the asset to a custodian “as soon as reasonably practicable” once one appears — with private-key protection, cybersecurity controls and strict segregation of client holdings in place throughout. State-chartered trust companies get a lane too, conditioned on state authorisation, loss-prevention procedures, audited financials, internal-control reports and segregated client assets. Read that list again as a list rather than a rule, because it is the clearest public statement anyone has made about what holding keys for other people requires. Nearly every line is about the gap between your assets and somebody else’s: segregation, authorisation, loss prevention, the audit that proves it. A person holding their own keys does not have that gap. There is no commingling question when there is no client. The institutions are being handed a long and expensive version of a problem retail solved by not creating it — and a 60-day comment window, starting when the proposal hits the Federal Register, to argue about the price.

01 — Fintech pulse
Three regulators spent the last week writing down what a dollar token is. None of the answers are interesting, which is the entire achievement.
The Federal Reserve has proposed rules implementing the GENIUS Act stablecoin framework for state member banks — which activities are permissible, reserves held in cash and short-term Treasuries, how fast a holder must be able to redeem, and standardised capital treatment. The SEC, separately, issued guidance easing capital rules in a direction that moves stablecoins closer to cash status for the firms holding them. And on October 1 the Banque de France published guidance whose main job is to stop people confusing a privately issued stablecoin with central-bank digital money, on the grounds that monetary sovereignty depends on the distinction. September was the month US stablecoin and tokenised-asset policy stopped being legislation and became rulemaking: comment windows, reserve schedules, redemption deadlines. Dull as a plumbing inspection, and about as load-bearing.
What all three documents are really arguing about is the same question a user asks without words: when I move this, is it done? Reserve rules and redemption deadlines exist so the answer can be yes without a phone call. That is the standard we hold ourselves to on the rails we already run, and it is deliberately unglamorous. SwopPay checkout charges 0.5%, and the rate is the same on the card rail and the crypto rail — one number, not a headline with a quieter one behind it. Swop covers the network fee rather than passing it through; it is a cost absorbed, not a line item. Crypto and x402 payments are never identity-gated, so a seller is payable in USDC the moment they list a product — only the card rail requires merchant verification, because the card networks require it there. On that rail the card proceeds never touch a Swop wallet at all: Stripe holds the fiat balance and pays the merchant’s bank. None of that is a feature anyone screenshots. It is just what has to be true before a payment is allowed to be boring.

02 — From the Journal
Sell digital products for crypto: how it works
Newest on the Swop Journal: selling a digital product for crypto means pricing it in a stablecoin, listing it somewhere a wallet can pay directly, and getting paid the moment the payment settles on-chain — no processor holding the funds, no payout schedule to wait out. The buyer signs from their own wallet instead of typing a card number, and increasingly the buyer is software: an AI agent paying over x402, with no checkout form to fill in at all. What changes versus a conventional digital-goods platform is custody and speed — the money lands in a wallet the seller controls, not in a merchant account somebody else is holding on their behalf.
The post also says the part most storefront pitches skip: settling the payment and delivering the file are two different problems. A platform can get the on-chain settlement exactly right and still leave the actual handover to a manual DM, so the question to ask before you run a real sale through anything is whether delivery fires automatically off the payment or depends on you remembering to follow up. That is the whole test for a money path — does it work when nobody is watching it. Also on the Journal: what is a wrapped token — a claim on a bridge, not on the original issuer.
