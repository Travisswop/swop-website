Today's big story
September was the most expensive month crypto has had this year, and 92% of the bill came from two places users could not look inside.
The month-end tallies are in and they agree on the shape if not the last digit: PeckShield puts September’s hack and exploit losses above $766M, roughly a 462% jump on August’s $136.3M and the worst month of 2026. Incident counts vary by tracker — 55 by one count, closer to 97 by CertiK’s — but the concentration does not. Two breaches account for about $707M, around 92% of the total. Bitget lost roughly $388M after attackers exploited a flaw in a third-party security product and used it to push forged transactions through its own approval flow; hot and warm wallets drained, cold wallets untouched. Liquid Network lost roughly $320M to a code flaw that let attackers mint nearly 4,000 fake L-BTC, drawing its bitcoin reserves down to 197 BTC, of which more than $270M has since been returned. Sit with what neither attack was. Nobody broke cryptography and nobody guessed a user’s key. One broke an approval system; the other broke an accounting rule about how much of an asset is allowed to exist. Both are failures in a layer the depositor cannot inspect, cannot audit, and cannot opt out of once the coins are inside. That is the recurring lesson of 2026, and it is not about choosing a safer exchange.

01 — Fintech pulse
Europe switched on a way to settle tokenised trades in central-bank money, no stablecoin required.
On September 21 the Eurosystem launched Pontes, the near-term track of the ECB’s two-track distributed-ledger plan — Appia is the long-term one. Pontes links market DLT platforms to the ECB’s TARGET Services so the cash leg of a tokenised trade settles in central-bank money: a participant can settle on the Eurosystem DLT platform using cash tokens, or in T2, the real-time gross settlement system, with finality reached once that cash leg completes in T2. It opens with 13 financial institutions and four DLT operators, and wider functionality is targeted for 2028. The ECB also said it will put part of its own funds into tokenised securities and settle them through Pontes, which is a central bank agreeing to be a customer of its own plumbing — a more convincing endorsement than a consultation paper.
Set that beside Washington, where the Fed has just proposed what private stablecoin issuers may hold in reserve and how fast they must redeem, and you have two jurisdictions answering the same question in opposite directions: Frankfurt is keeping the cash leg inside central-bank money, Washington is regulating the private dollar that replaced it. The detail that matters to anyone building a payment flow is neither rulebook. It is that both are answers to “when is this final?” — and settlement finality is the entire reason a money path can be boring. A payment that settles and stays settled needs no status page, no support ticket, and no explanation. That is the standard we hold ourselves to on the rails we already run: Swop covers the network fee rather than passing it through, crypto and x402 payments are never identity-gated, and a seller is payable in USDC the moment they list a product. Only the card rail asks for merchant verification, because the card networks require it there.

02 — From the Journal
Sell digital products for crypto: how it works
New on the Swop Journal today: selling a digital product for crypto means pricing it in a stablecoin, listing it somewhere a wallet can pay directly, and getting paid the moment the payment settles on-chain — no processor holding the funds, no payout schedule to wait out. The buyer signs from their own wallet instead of typing a card number, and increasingly the buyer is software: an AI agent paying over x402, with no checkout form to fill in at all. What changes versus a conventional digital-goods platform is custody and speed — the money lands in a wallet the seller controls, not in a merchant account somebody else is holding on their behalf.
The post also says the part most storefront pitches skip: settling the payment and delivering the file are two different problems. A platform can get the on-chain settlement exactly right and still leave the actual handover to a manual DM, so the question to ask before you run a real sale through anything is whether delivery fires automatically off the payment or depends on you remembering to follow up. That is the whole test for a money path — does it work without you watching it. Also on the Journal: what is a wrapped token — a claim on a bridge, not on the original issuer.
