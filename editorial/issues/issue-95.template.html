Today's big story
Ethereum’s exit queue has started to drain. The queue to get back in is still nearly twice its size.
A week ago MetaMask disclosed that part of its staking infrastructure had been compromised and began a precautionary mass exit of its validators. The queue that created peaked around 851,000 ETH on October 2, printed 786,275 ETH yesterday with an estimated wait of 13 days and 16 hours — the longest of 2026 — and has since eased back to roughly 767,000 ETH. The protocol expects the last of the affected MetaMask validators to stop staking by October 7, and an independent estimate puts the affected stake near 523,000 ETH across about 17,000 validators, a figure MetaMask has not confirmed. So the exit is close to finished and the backlog is receding. Here is the number that is not receding: live queue data shows about 1.46 million ETH waiting to enter, with activation taking more than 25 days. The way back in is nearly twice as long as the way out was, and the whole round trip is estimated at about 45 days. Lido, for its part, expects most of the exiting ETH to be restaked rather than sold — which is the tell that this was never a verdict on Ethereum. It was one operator’s incident propagating through a queue with a fixed throughput, and the people who left mostly want back in. Keep the shape of it: an incident has two durations, the compromise and the recovery, and the recovery is the one measured in weeks of somebody else’s capital sitting idle. That is a retention number wearing a protocol’s clothes. Elsewhere on the same network, the Glamsterdam upgrade reaches Sepolia today, with a fourth-quarter target and no confirmed mainnet date.

01 — Fintech pulse
Custody spent the week being argued in three venues. Stripe spent it shipping a card to a hundred countries.
Two things happened in the same week and only one of them will be argued over. Stripe said it expects its stablecoin card programmes to run in more than a hundred countries by the end of the year, and handed oversight of stablecoins and crypto across the company to Henri Stern, chief executive of the wallet-infrastructure firm Privy that Stripe bought in 2025. Spending on stablecoin cards roughly tripled over the past year; the named customers are Kraken, the fintech Ramp and the payments app Morse; and the company says it is also looking at tokenised deposits and DeFi, with stablecoins still the centre of it. Meanwhile the legal question underneath all of it moved in three directions at once. On October 1 the SEC proposed its custody framework, and the shape matters more than the arrival: two paths, not one. Registered advisers, registered investment companies and business development companies could rely on state trust companies as custodians, subject to checks on a company’s authority and its safeguards — and, where no permitted custodian is available, could hold the assets themselves under conditions. Chair Paul Atkins said the Commission had “proposed to close a gap that has left investment advisers and funds guessing how to effect lawful custody,” adding that “our work is not finished.” Comments run 60 days from Federal Register publication. The day after, the Independent Community Bankers of America sued the OCC in the District of Columbia under the Administrative Procedure Act, asking a court to void the national trust chartering rule published in the Federal Register on March 2 and the 2021 Interpretive Letter 1176 it is built on — the one path crypto firms had found into the federal banking stack. Rebeca Romero Rainey’s framing was that “Congress did not create the national trust charter as a side door into the banking system.”
Strip the venues away and every one of those documents argues about a single sentence: the conditions under which somebody may hold an asset that belongs to someone else. Segregation, consolidated supervision, insurance, the audit that proves it — each requirement exists to police the gap between your property and an institution’s balance sheet. Self-custody does not win that argument; it declines to enter it, because there is no commingling question where there is no custodian. What that leaves is the unglamorous half: whether the money path itself works. SwopPay checkout charges 0.5%, and the rate is the same on the card rail and the crypto rail — one number, not a headline with a quieter one behind it. Swop covers the network fee rather than passing it through; a cost absorbed, not a line item. Crypto and x402 payments are never identity-gated, so a seller is payable in USDC the moment they list a product — only the card rail requires merchant verification, because the card networks require it there. On that rail the proceeds never touch a Swop wallet at all: Stripe holds the fiat balance and pays the merchant’s bank. None of that is a feature anyone screenshots. It is just what has to be true before a payment is allowed to be boring.

02 — From the Journal
The unbanked don’t need a bank. They need a ledger.
Published on the Swop Journal today, and it reframes the custody argument above from the other end. Two numbers from the same World Bank survey: 1.3 billion adults have no financial account, and 86 per cent of adults own a mobile phone. Whatever has kept a billion people out of the financial system, it has not been the absence of a device — it is arithmetic. Verifying and servicing an account costs roughly the same whether the balance is forty dollars or forty thousand, so below a certain threshold every account is a small recurring loss, and the exclusion is a spreadsheet result rather than a decision anyone made.
What changed is not access but what a wallet can hold. Tokenisation put dollars in one through stablecoins, short-term Treasuries through Ondo’s USDY, and one troy ounce of allocated LBMA gold through PAX Gold — so the pitch stops being “hold this volatile asset” and becomes “hold what wealthy people hold to store value, in an account nobody had to approve.” The post is equally direct about what a keypair still cannot do: no credit, no recourse, no chargeback, and key loss remains real, with recovery the weakest part of this industry. Any version of the argument that skips that is selling something. Also on the Journal: selling digital products for crypto — settlement and delivery are two different problems.

03 — Day ahead
What to watch — the trade gap already in, two Fed voices today, a pair of National League Game 3s tonight, and Wednesday’s minutes still doing the heavy lifting.
📅 Macro Calendar
• Today, 8:30 ET — printed — the August trade deficit widened to 105.6 billion dollars, past the roughly 102 billion consensus, on record imports of 420.8 billion. Goods imports rose 5.3 per cent, led by capital goods — semiconductors and industrial machinery. That is the AI build-out showing up in the trade data, which makes the widening a capital-spending story rather than evidence about tariffs either way
• Still today — the weekly ADP employment change, and two Fed voices in one session: governor Michelle Bowman and New York’s John Williams. Two speakers on the same day is where a split tends to show before any minutes confirm it. Atlanta Fed’s GDPNow and the API crude stocks update as well
• Wednesday, 14:00 ET — minutes from the Federal Reserve’s September 15–16 meeting. With no CPI until October 14, the minutes are the week’s main event, and the question is how much disagreement the vote concealed. Waller follows on Thursday, Collins on Friday
• The lens — traders priced out an October hike over the weekend, so the minutes get read as confirmation or contradiction of a position the market has already taken. That is a different thing from the minutes settling it
⚖ Court Watch
• ICBA v. OCC, filed October 2 — the community bankers’ Administrative Procedure Act challenge in the US District Court for the District of Columbia, aimed at the OCC’s March 2 final rule tied to Interpretive Letter 1176 and the three conditional trust charters granted on September 18. A live complaint, not a ruling, and no hearing date yet
• Prediction markets, still the petition pool — the Ninth Circuit held on August 28 that sports event contracts are not swaps within the CFTC’s exclusive jurisdiction, declining to preempt Nevada’s gaming law and splitting with the Third Circuit. Robinhood petitioned the Supreme Court on September 9 and Crypto.com’s Nadex on September 11; Kalshi is separately seeking en banc rehearing. No cert grant, no argument date
• CFTC v. KalshiEX — a Rule 16 scheduling conference in the Massachusetts case is reported for October 13, and the agency’s prediction-market rulemaking sits at the White House for review. Rules landing while the circuits disagree can moot half the fight or sharpen it, and which one will be obvious from the text
• SEC custody proposal — the comment window runs 60 days from Federal Register publication. The comments worth reading will come from the firms that would rather be the qualified custodian than compete with people who hold their own keys
🎯 Tonight's Games · Swop Predictions vs. the Books
• Two National League Game 3s — Dodgers at Braves 6:00 ET on Fox, Brewers at Padres 9:30 ET on FS1. Los Angeles and Atlanta are level at a win apiece; Milwaukee is reported to lead San Diego two games to none. In the American League, Tampa Bay and Chicago are each reported to hold two-nil leads in their series
• Why these are two different prices — a tied series and a two-nil series are not the same bet wearing different numbers. At 1–1 in a best-of-five, Game 3 is close to the whole series in one night, and the price should look like it. At 2–0 the market has to weigh two results against a team that has not yet had to win anything, which is where a series price is most often wrong in both directions at once. A book reprices to balance its exposure; a prediction market reprices because the probability changed
• Check it rather than trust it — pull the live series prices in the app before first pitch and compare them to a sportsbook’s. The point of an order book is that you do not have to take our word for it
A book quotes a price and manages its exposure; a prediction market quotes the probability and reprices as the series moves. The divergence is the thing to look at.
