The CLARITY Act cloture vote failed 49–50 yesterday — eleven short of the 60 required, and one short of even a simple majority. The reflex reading is that crypto just lost its shot at rules. That isn't what happened, and the real situation is more worth understanding.

First, it didn't break on the market-structure framework. It broke on ethics language covering officials' crypto holdings. No voting Democrat supported the motion and four Republicans opposed it.

Second, the agencies never waited. The SEC's Project Crypto has been building a token taxonomy, and in August it proposed Regulation Crypto Assets — a tailored offering regime the Commission says is partly intended to reduce the incentive for issuers to organize offshore. The CFTC has gone further in practice: listed spot crypto products began trading on federally regulated U.S. markets in December 2025, and a joint release in March named sixteen assets as digital commodities. Anyone claiming the U.S. has no crypto policy is describing 2022.

So why does legislation still matter? Because a rule and a statute can say identical things and be worth different amounts to the person deciding where to put money or where to incorporate. A future commission can supersede a rule. A court can vacate it. Congress can disapprove it. A proposal can simply never be finalized. None of that requires bad faith — it is ordinary administrative law working as designed. Changing a statute takes both chambers and a signature.

That difference is priced. A fund committing to a ten-year vehicle underwrites a regulatory regime across a horizon longer than any single administration, and new venture fund formation just hit its lowest quarterly total since 2020. Founders choose a jurisdiction against the rules they expect at exit, not the rules today — which is why offshore structuring persisted through a friendlier turn in Washington.

And the property most worth making durable is self-custody. The World Bank's Global Findex 2025 counts 1.3 billion adults with no financial account, while 86% of adults own a mobile phone. That gap is not technological, it is permissional: a self-custodial wallet is an account nobody has to grant you. Meanwhile $33 trillion in stablecoin transfers settled on public chains in 2025, up 72% year over year, and 71% of Latin American firms now use them for cross-border settlement, while remittances into Sub-Saharan Africa still average over 6% in fees. People are not waiting for permission to join a better rail.

Which is why Section 604 — whether publishing non-custodial software makes you a money transmitter — is the stake that matters most. Settled in guidance, it can be resettled. Leaving it unresolved protects no one; it exports the work.
