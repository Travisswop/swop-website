Crypto's favourite proof that tokenized money works is remittances. And the transfer leg really is solved: a stablecoin crosses a border in seconds for a fraction of a cent. But a remittance isn't finished when money crosses a border. It's finished when someone can spend it — and that half of the trip hasn't moved.

"Remittance" always described one trip doing two jobs: move the value across the border, and hand over something spendable at the other end. Historically one rail did both, because nothing could separate them. A stablecoin transfer separates them for the first time — and it only does the first job. A balance of tokenized dollars in a wallet is not the same thing as cash in a recipient's hand or a deposit their bank will accept, and conflating those two is where "crypto fixed remittances" overreaches.

Credit where it's due first, because the gap is real. The World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide database tracks 377 country corridors, from 48 sending to 111 receiving countries, and puts the global average cost of sending $200 at 6.36% in its most recent published round (Q3 2025 data). A September 2026 Solana Foundation report on US corridors cites 6.49% and three to five business days.

One caveat on that second figure, because it matters: 6.49% is the World Bank's own global average read one quarter earlier. Same series, different point. It corroborates nothing independently, and the publisher has an evident interest in the comparison — its settlement-time range is reported as its own claim, not something independently verified. The underlying gap is real either way.

What's left is the last mile. Most recipients need local cash, or a deposit their own bank or mobile-money account will accept, because that is what pays rent. Getting from a wallet balance to that point requires an off-ramp: a local agent, exchange or payout partner with local liquidity, compliance work already done, and operating hours in the receiving country. None of that moved on-chain. Only the transfer between the two ends of it did — so the fee and the delay that used to sit across the whole trip now concentrate almost entirely on that final leg.

The clearest evidence is what the incumbents actually did. Western Union launched USDPT on Solana in May 2026; MoneyGram launched MGUSD on Stellar in June 2026. Both are positioned first as settlement assets, moving dollars across their own networks around the clock instead of waiting on correspondent banks. And both go further than back-end plumbing — MoneyGram's balance sits in a self-custodial wallet inside its own app, letting a recipient hold dollars rather than convert immediately. That cuts against the lazy version of this argument, so it's worth stating plainly.

But look at what neither of them retired. MoneyGram's dollar balance still converts on demand across roughly 500,000 retail locations, because cash is what a recipient who needs cash actually needs. The wire between their own accounts got replaced. The payout network got kept — and the payout network, not the wire, is the expensive thing to run.

So the test case isn't failed. It's conditional. When the recipient is willing and able to hold the stablecoin itself, there is no second leg, because the transfer is the payment: a freelancer invoicing in USDC, a merchant settling with a cross-border supplier, a saver who already keeps a dollar-denominated balance. For them tokenized money passes outright — instant, near-free, and finished the moment it arrives. For a recipient who needs local cash at a counter tomorrow, it solved the cheap half of the problem and left the expensive half alone.

Where we sit, stated as one leg rather than the whole trip: Swop is fully self-custodial — keys are generated and held on your device; Swop never holds them. Transactions are gas-sponsored, so neither side needs to hold SOL or ETH to move money, and it runs on Solana, Ethereum, Base and Polygon. It is not a cash-out network, and we're not going to pretend otherwise.

Whether a given remittance also needs a cash-out depends on whether the recipient can use a dollar-denominated balance directly. That's the real question, and it isn't one any wallet answers just by existing.
