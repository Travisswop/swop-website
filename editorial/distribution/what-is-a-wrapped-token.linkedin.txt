A wrapped token carries the same ticker as the asset it represents and is designed to track the same price. What it does not carry is the same backing — and that gap is completely invisible in a wallet balance.

What it actually is: a token minted on one blockchain to represent an asset that lives on another. A bridge or a custodian locks the original on its native chain and mints a matching token on the destination chain, redeemable one-for-one in principle.

The part that matters is who does the issuing. The wrapped version is created by whoever holds that lock — not by the protocol or company behind the original asset. Holding wBTC is not holding Bitcoin. It is holding a claim, from a specific issuer, that they will give you Bitcoin. Most of the time that distinction costs you nothing. It is not most of the time that you need to think about.

The mechanics are the same whether a centralised custodian or a decentralised bridge is doing it. Lock the original. Mint an equivalent amount elsewhere. Burn the wrapped token to redeem. Total wrapped supply is meant to always equal the amount locked — and "meant to" is doing real work in that sentence, because it is an accounting invariant maintained by the issuer rather than a property the chain enforces.

The naming convention is a giveaway once you know to look: a "w" prefix — wBTC, wETH, wSOL — usually signals a wrapped representation rather than the native asset. Usually. It is a convention nobody enforces, which is exactly why the contract address matters more than the ticker.

So the useful question is never "is this the real asset." It isn't. The question is who holds the lock, whether the reserves are independently verifiable, and what happens to your claim if that party fails. It is the same concentration risk that makes bridges the most-exploited component in crypto: the wrapper sits downstream of whatever secures the lock.

None of which means avoid wrapped tokens. A great deal of DeFi runs on them, and they are why assets can move between chains at all. It means reading the ticker as a label rather than a guarantee, and knowing whose promise you are holding.
