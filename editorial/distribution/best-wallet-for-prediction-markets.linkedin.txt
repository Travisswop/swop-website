When people compare apps for prediction markets, they compare the odds. But the odds are largely the same everywhere — they are set by the order book, not by the app. What actually differs is everything wrapped around the trade.

Custody comes first. Is the app self-custodial, or is your position sitting in a pooled balance whose keys you do not hold? A winning contract that resolves correctly and cannot be withdrawn is not a win. That question has nothing to do with which market you chose, and everything to do with whether you own the outcome.

Then gas. Some apps require you to acquire a separate native token before you can place a single trade — a genuine barrier dressed up as a technicality. You arrived to price an outcome, not to first buy a different asset for the privilege of doing so.

Then depth, which is where most comparisons stop too early. A yes/no share trading at 62 cents means the market is saying 62%. The useful question is whether you can actually be filled at 62 cents, or whether your size walks the book. Seeing full order-book depth before you commit funds is the difference between a quoted price and your price.

Then fees, and specifically where they sit: in the spread, in a per-trade charge, or in the withdrawal. All three are legitimate. Undisclosed is not — you should be able to see the number before committing rather than infer it from what arrived.

And if the app ships an AI trading agent, one question settles it: does the agent propose and wait for you, or is it permitted to act on its own? An agent that can open a position without your signature is a fundamentally different product from one that drafts a trade and asks.

None of this is exotic. These are the same questions worth asking of any wallet. They simply matter more once real money is riding on an outcome that will resolve.
