Two numbers from the same World Bank survey. 1.3 billion adults have no account at a bank, a financial institution, or a mobile money provider. 86% of adults worldwide own a mobile phone.

Put those side by side and the usual explanation falls apart. Whatever is keeping a billion people out of the financial system, it is not that they lack a device, and it has not been for years.

The honest answer is less comfortable than a technology gap. It is arithmetic. Opening an account requires identity verification, sanctions screening and ongoing compliance monitoring, and those costs are close to fixed — verifying a customer with forty dollars costs roughly what it costs to verify one with forty thousand. Add servicing and overhead and the economics only work above a balance threshold. Below it, every account is a small recurring loss. Nobody is being excluded out of malice. They are being excluded by a spreadsheet.

Mobile money moved that threshold down considerably, which is why account ownership has climbed for fifteen years. But it moved the threshold; it did not remove it. There is still an institution deciding whether you clear a bar.

A self-custodial wallet is a keypair. Issuing one more costs effectively nothing, requires nobody's approval, and carries no minimum balance. The economics that excluded a billion people simply stop applying. Whether the resulting thing is useful is a separate question — and for most of crypto's history, the honest answer was "not very." Telling someone living on an uncertain income that they can now hold an asset that might fall 40% in a month is not financial inclusion. It is a different kind of exclusion with better branding.

That is what tokenization changed. Stablecoins put dollars in the wallet. Tokenized Treasuries put government debt in it — Ondo's USDY runs to roughly $2.1 billion, backed mainly by short-term US Treasuries. Tokenized gold puts allocated metal in it: each PAX Gold token represents one fine troy ounce of LBMA Good Delivery gold vaulted in London. Tokenized equities put index exposure in it.

So the proposition stops being "hold this volatile asset" and becomes "hold the same instruments wealthy people use to store value, in an account nobody had to approve." That is a materially different argument, and it is the one that makes financial inclusion serious rather than aspirational. It is also already happening: roughly $33 trillion in stablecoin transfers settled on public blockchains in 2025, up 72% year over year.

What a wallet still cannot do, stated plainly: it extends no credit, offers no recourse, and has no chargeback. And key loss is real — recovery design remains the weakest part of this story industry-wide. Any version of "self-custody banks the unbanked" that treats that as a footnote is selling something.

Which is why the honest framing is a ledger entry you control, rather than a bank in your pocket. It does fewer things than a bank. It does them for everyone.
