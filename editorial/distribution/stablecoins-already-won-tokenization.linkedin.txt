Tokenization gets pitched as something coming for Treasuries, gold and real estate. By the only metric that counts — value actually moved on-chain — it already happened, years ago, and the asset that won was the dollar.

A stablecoin is tokenization. A token issued against a reserve of dollars or dollar-equivalents held off-chain, redeemable against that reserve, transferable on a public blockchain. That is the same structure as tokenized gold or a tokenized Treasury fund — not a metaphor, but the literal shape every tracker uses when it adds up "tokenized real-world assets." Under that structure, stablecoins aren't kept in a separate bucket from Treasuries and gold. They are the biggest line item inside the same bucket.

The numbers, which rarely get stated side by side: CoinDesk Data's July 2026 report put total stablecoin market capitalisation at roughly $308 billion, against $32.1 billion for non-stablecoin tokenized real-world assets — and that second figure was the highest it had ever reached. A Token Terminal breakdown from the same quarter put total on-chain tokenized value near $346 billion, with stablecoins about 86% of it and the remainder, led by tokenized Treasuries at around $15 billion, split across every other category.

Figures differ by tracker and by week, because different trackers include different token sets and measure distributed value differently from represented value. I'd rather state that than imply a precision the data doesn't have. But every major source lands in the same place.

The rest of tokenization is not standing still either. CoinGecko's research desk recorded tokenized RWAs excluding stablecoins growing 256.7% over fifteen months, from $5.42 billion in January 2025 to $19.32 billion by the end of Q1 2026. That is a real growth rate by any standard. It is also, on CoinGecko's own framing, still only about 6.4% the size of the stablecoin market, up from 2.7% a year earlier — fast growth off a small base. Both halves of that sentence matter.

Why did stablecoins win first? Not timing — a structural head start. Demand for dollars is already universal, so a stablecoin doesn't have to create a market, only move an existing one onto a rail that settles faster and crosses borders more easily. Tokenized Treasuries and gold have more work to do before they can even compete: most are gated to accredited or institutional buyers, and most route through a transfer-agent or allowlist layer that decides who is permitted to hold the token at all. A token that reverts if it lands in the wrong wallet is solving a compliance problem before it can solve a convenience problem.

There's a simpler reason too. Most people who want gold or Treasury exposure can already get it through a brokerage account in a few taps, so tokenization has to beat an incumbent that is already easy to use. Cross-border dollar access is the opposite case — for a large share of the world the existing option is genuinely bad, and that is the gap stablecoins stepped into.

Now where the analogy stops, because "winning" doesn't mean what it's often taken to mean. Holding a stablecoin means holding a claim on an issuer's promise to redeem it, backed by a reserve that issuer controls and reports on. That claim is only as good as the reserve, the audits behind it, and the issuer's own solvency — the identical counterparty question that applies to a tokenized Treasury fund or a tokenized gold bar. Tokenization moved the asset onto a faster, more transferable rail. It did not remove the issuer sitting in the middle of the claim, and it did not make every stablecoin interchangeable: reserve composition, audit cadence and jurisdictional backstops all differ between issuers.

To be explicit, since this is a market-structure observation and not a pitch: this is about tokenization broadly, not a claim about any stablecoin Swop issues. Swop doesn't issue one.

Where we do sit is the self-custody side of the question. Swop is fully self-custodial — keys are generated and held on your device; Swop never holds them — and it runs on Solana, Ethereum, Base and Polygon, so stablecoins on any of those chains sit in a wallet you control rather than an account someone else administers. Transactions are gas-sponsored, which matters here because moving a stablecoin you already hold shouldn't require first acquiring a second asset just to pay the fee.

What the next wave of tokenization borrows from stablecoins is the part that actually mattered: an asset you can hold yourself, move without asking permission, and settle instantly — rather than one merely described on a blockchain while the real custody arrangement stays exactly as it was.
