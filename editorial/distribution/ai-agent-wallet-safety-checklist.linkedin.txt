Every wallet that has added an AI feature now describes itself as "safe." That word is doing a lot of work, and almost none of it is checkable from a product page.

The useful reframing is that trust isn't the thing to evaluate — architecture is. An agent's intentions don't matter if the system around it can't enforce a limit. So here are the five mechanisms that actually decide the answer, in the order they matter.

Self-custody: keys generated and held only on your device, not by the agent and not by the company running it. Confirmation before signing: the agent drafts a proposal with the amounts and the reasoning, and waits for a tap — an agent trade should not get a shortcut around the gate a manual trade goes through. Bounded authority rather than alerts: a notification after a large trade is not a safeguard; a policy requiring your cryptographic sign-off before the spending cap or position size widens is. An audit trail per decision, logged so it cannot be quietly edited afterward. And an exit that is never blocked — closing a position or withdrawing to yourself must never be something the policy layer can trap you out of.

Where Swop lands, measured against its own checklist: self-custodial, agent proposes and you approve, per-vault hash-chained audit log, exits always open. The policy layer shipped on August 28 but is still running in shadow (observe-and-log) mode while it soaks — so by the standard above it belongs on the "reported" side of the line rather than the "enforced" side until that flips. We also have not commissioned a third-party security audit, and we say so rather than implying one exists.

That kind of precision is the whole point. The checklist only works if wallets describe their own mechanisms accurately, including ours.
