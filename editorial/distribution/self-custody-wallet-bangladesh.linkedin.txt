"Self-custody crypto wallet Bangladesh" is a real search with a real reason behind it. It also has an answer that isn't the one a wallet company would prefer to give.

Start with what self-custody actually is. A custodial wallet — the kind built into most exchanges — holds the private key for you, which means the exchange can freeze your account, reverse a mistaken transfer, or act on a court order from its own database. A self-custody wallet generates the key on your device and never transmits it. The tradeoff is symmetrical: nobody can do those things for you either, including undo a mistake.

That is the entire distinction. It says nothing about which chain you are on, which country you are in, or whether the underlying activity is permitted. It is purely a statement about where the key lives.

The structural gap behind the search is genuine. Bangladesh is among the world's largest remittance markets, and the 2025 Global Findex still counts it among the eight countries holding more than half of the world's 650 million unbanked adults. The bank channel is also expensive: globally, banks average 9.50% on remittances against 3.65% for digital providers. Though it is worth being accurate rather than convenient — South Asia is actually the cheapest major destination region at 5.18%, partly because competition on the India, Pakistan and Bangladesh routes is intense. So the honest framing is not "Bangladesh is expensive," it is that the bank channel is expensive nearly everywhere while account access remains uneven.

None of which answers the legal question, and the legal question is the one that governs. Bangladesh Bank has stated repeatedly that trading or transacting in cryptocurrency is not permitted in the country, citing the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act 1947, the Money Laundering Prevention Act 2012 and the Anti-Terrorism Act 2009. That restriction applies to the activity rather than to a wallet type, so there is no self-custodial exception to it. Holding your own keys does not make a restricted transaction permitted, any more than holding physical cash makes an otherwise-illegal cash transaction legal.

We would rather say that plainly than imply a workaround. The law where you are is ours to follow, not to route around — check Bangladesh Bank's current published guidance before doing anything, and treat this as context rather than legal advice.
