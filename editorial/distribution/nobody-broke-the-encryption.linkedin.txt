Almost nothing that got breached this year was breached by defeating cryptography. Nobody factored a key or broke a cipher. They phished an employee, exploited an unpatched server, stole a live session, or had a password-reset link sent to an inbox they already controlled. The maths held. The way we prove who we are did not.

Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, covering November 2024 to October 2025, contains a finding worth sitting with: 31% of breaches now start with a software vulnerability, which Verizon describes as having overtaken stolen passwords as the top way attackers get in. That cuts against the lazy version of this argument, so it's worth saying plainly — "passwords are the whole problem" is no longer true at the point of entry. Credentials remain enormous, appearing somewhere in roughly 39% of breaches. But the front door changed.

What's absent from every one of those lists is a row for "defeated the encryption." It effectively does not happen. The cryptography is the one component performing to specification. Everything failing around it is authentication, patching and human judgement. We spend our anxiety on the part of the stack that is working.

The structural problem with the login model most of the internet still runs on is this: whatever you use to sign in, there is almost always a path that ends at your email. Forgotten password, magic link, one-time code. That makes your inbox the root of trust for everything else you own — and an inbox is a shared-secret account, reachable from anywhere, recoverable by a support process, protected by a password and perhaps an SMS code a SIM swap can redirect. Email was designed for trusted networks. It became the custodian of every other account you hold by accident, because it was the one identifier everybody already had. Your security is therefore not the strength of your strongest login; it's the strength of your weakest recovery path.

The alternative is older than the problem: stop sharing secrets. You keep a private key, the service keeps the matching public key, and you prove who you are by signing a challenge. The service never holds anything an attacker can use. That's what passkeys do — and a passkey signature is bound to the site that requested it, so a convincing fake cannot replay it. That removes phishing as a mechanism rather than asking people to be better at spotting fakes. Structurally it is also exactly what a crypto wallet is: a key pair with a balance attached, where signing a transaction and signing a login challenge are the same operation. The industry arrived at the same answer twice, independently, for the same reason — the only secret that cannot be stolen from a server is one the server never had.

So why do legacy systems keep losing this fight? Not because they're old. Because they are custodial by design, and custody creates an override. If a provider can restore your access when you lose it, a procedure exists that transfers control of your account to whoever completes it convincingly. That override is the feature people rely on most and the most reliable thing to attack, and it can't be fully patched, because removing it means telling a locked-out customer that nothing can be done. Every large consumer platform resolves that tension in favour of recoverability — which is why social engineering keeps working against companies with excellent engineering.

A blockchain account is structurally different rather than incrementally better. The account is the key pair. No administrator, so no override to socially engineer, no reset flow to subvert, and no credential database to breach because there is no database. It is also unforgiving, and the honest version says so in the same breath: when a key is gone, it is gone. The real task isn't choosing between the two models. It's building recovery that doesn't reintroduce a skeleton key.

Which brings me to the claim doing the most work in crypto security marketing right now. Ethereum is not quantum-resistant today. Account signatures use elliptic-curve cryptography that a sufficiently large quantum computer would break, and Google Quantum AI research in March 2026 estimated roughly 1,200 logical qubits to break 256-bit elliptic curve cryptography. The Ethereum Foundation's post-quantum roadmap is serious and public — signature abstraction at the account level, hash-based signatures replacing validator BLS, proof systems for the far larger signatures — and it targets core infrastructure around 2029. Real work, credible people, right direction. And close to nothing to do with the breaches above. Post-quantum cryptography defends against a future adversary who can break today's maths; it does nothing about a phishing page, an unpatched server or a stolen session. Anyone selling quantum resistance as the answer to the current security crisis is answering a different question on a different clock.

Wallets don't fix everything either. Moving to keys deletes one category of attack and creates two: you can lose the key permanently, and attackers followed. The dominant attack on wallet users isn't cracking keys, it's persuading people to sign something. Phishing moved from "give me your password" to "approve this" — harder to spot, because what you're approving is often unreadable.

And since a post arguing email is the weak link should say where its author sits: Swop is fully self-custodial, keys are generated and held on your device, Swop never holds them, and wallets run on Privy today. You can export your private key, which is the only recovery path here that doesn't depend on a company, an inbox or a password manager. Swop supports sign-in passkeys — but adding one does not switch email off. It adds a second door rather than closing the first, which by itself is a convenience upgrade rather than a security boundary. That isn't a Swop quirk; it's how most passkey rollouts quietly deliver less than they appear to.

If you use Swop, the setting that actually changes your exposure is Transaction Security in Wallet Settings — passkey verification on transactions. It's separate from sign-in passkeys and it's off unless you turn it on. With it on, someone who gets into your account still can't move funds without your passkey. For completeness: Swop has not commissioned a third-party security audit to date.

What we owe people is the same thing the industry owes them — recovery that doesn't reintroduce a skeleton key. A passkey that makes email insufficient rather than merely optional, step-up verification on the actions that move value, and a real self-custody backstop for anyone who wants out of trusting a provider at all. The last exists today. The first two are the work.
