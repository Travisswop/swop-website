El Dorado is live in beta at swopme.co/eldorado. It is a virtual world built on Swop.

We shipped it rather than announced it, so the honest version of this post is short on promises and clear about what exists today. Here is what it is, what makes it structurally different from the virtual worlds you have used before, and what it is not yet.

What El Dorado is

A virtual world that runs on Swop’s rails. That second half is the part worth paying attention to, and it is the reason this post exists on a blog that mostly argues about settlement and custody rather than about games.

The piece we published yesterday made the case that “tokenized” tells you almost nothing on its own — that the interesting variable is custody, and that it is set by whoever designs the thing, not by the chain underneath. El Dorado is what it looks like when you set that dial deliberately at the start, in a category where it has almost never been set that way at all.

You sign in with something you already hold

Open swopme.co/eldorado and you are asked to sign in with your swop.id from the Swop app. Not a new game account. Not a new password. The identity you already carry.

If you are going in on a headset, you do not type anything with a controller. Sign in on your phone first, then pair the headset — either by scanning a code using Wallet connections in the Swop app, or by opening the pairing page on your phone and entering the short code the headset displays. If you do not have a swop.id yet, you claim one in the Swop app and come back.

That flow is mundane to describe and it is the whole architectural point. The thing you authenticate with is a wallet whose keys live on your device. Swop is fully self-custodial — keys are generated and held on your device; Swop never holds them.

Every virtual economy has the same problem

Here is the pattern that has held across two decades of virtual worlds, and it has nothing to do with whether a given game is good.

In mainstream games, virtual items are granted under a limited licence. You are not buying property; you are buying permission to use something, and the terms usually say so explicitly. Publishers reserve the right to terminate an account, typically without refund. A ban does not just stop you playing — it freezes the inventory attached to that game, and an inventory you cannot reach has no practical value no matter what the market price says. Most publishers also prohibit transferring items or accounts outside their own systems, which means the exit is controlled by the same party as the entrance.

The sharpest illustration of this is not a story about an economy at all. It is a story about a city.

A group of developers spent years reverse-engineering Grand Theft Auto III and Vice City — rebuilding the games from the outside so they would run properly on modern machines. The projects, re3 and reVC, shipped no game content. You had to own the original game to use them at all. They were, in the most literal sense, a tool for people who had already paid.

In February 2021 Take-Two issued a DMCA notice and GitHub took the code down. A developer filed a counter-notice and a fork came back. A lawsuit followed that September, the projects came down again in October, and the following month Take-Two shipped its own remastered versions of both games. The case was later settled.

Today, if you open the original repository, GitHub returns HTTP 451 — Unavailable for Legal Reasons. We checked on the morning this post went up. A status code invented for censorship, named after Fahrenheit 451, returned for a rebuild of a two-decade-old game that you needed to own a copy of to run.

Reasonable people disagree about who was right there, and we are not relitigating it. The point is narrower and harder to argue with: owning the game was never the thing that mattered. Every player who bought it still bought a licence, and the city they could walk around in existed at the discretion of someone else. That is the default condition of every virtual world you have ever entered.

There are exceptions. EVE Online’s PLEX is a long-running sanctioned bridge between in-game currency and real money. But the exceptions prove the rule: they exist because a publisher chose to allow them, and could choose otherwise.

What a self-custodial wallet changes

Three things, concretely, and they follow from Swop’s existing properties rather than from anything novel we are asking you to take on faith.

Your key is yours. Keys are generated and held on your device; Swop never holds them. The practical consequence is that your access to what you hold does not depend on an account remaining in good standing with a single operator.

You do not need to pre-buy gas to do anything. Transactions on Swop are gas-sponsored — you do not need to hold SOL or ETH to transact, and Swop covers the network fee as a cost it absorbs rather than a charge passed through to you. For a virtual world this matters more than it sounds: the usual on-boarding failure is a new player bouncing off a screen demanding they acquire a second token before they can take a first action.

It is not confined to one world. Swop runs on Solana, Ethereum, Base and Polygon, and the wallet you sign in with is the same one you use everywhere else in Swop — not a per-game balance that only exists inside one company’s ledger.

And losing the phone is not losing the account: log in with your email on any phone and your Swop wallet comes back with it. You can also save your private key, which lets you open your assets in another wallet entirely.

What it doesn’t change

This is the section most product launch posts leave out, so we will be direct.

Self-custody is not the same as legal ownership. Holding an asset in a wallet you control changes what can be done to your access. It does not by itself settle what rights you have in a virtual good — that still depends on the terms you agreed to and on your jurisdiction, and the law here is genuinely unsettled and varies by country. Anyone telling you that a wallet converts a game item into property in the legal sense is skipping a step. We are claiming the custody half, which is real, and not the legal half, which is not ours to assert.

Self-custody also hands you the key problem. That is the trade. Email recovery and key export exist precisely because the honest version of “nobody can take it from you” is “nobody can get it back for you either.”

And Swop has not commissioned a third-party security audit to date. That is true of the platform El Dorado is built on, it is relevant to anyone deciding how much to put into a beta, and you should weigh it.

It’s a beta, and it moves weekly

El Dorado is in beta. We mean that literally rather than as a legal cushion: things will change under you, some of them will break, and features will move. Improvements roll out every week, so the version you walk into on a Monday is not the version that will be there the Monday after.

Beta is a description, not a disclaimer. Calling something a beta does not transfer the consequences of its rough edges onto the people using it. If something in El Dorado behaves badly, that is ours to fix, and the weekly cadence is how we intend to fix it in public rather than quietly.

If you want to see it, it is at swopme.co/eldorado. Bring a swop.id. If you do not have one, claim it in the Swop app first — and if you want the longer argument for why entering a virtual world with a wallet you control is different in kind from entering one with an account, yesterday’s post is the place to start.

FAQ

What is El Dorado?

A virtual world built on Swop. You enter it at swopme.co/eldorado by signing in with your swop.id — the same identity and self-custodial wallet you already use in the Swop app — rather than by creating a new game account. It is currently in beta.

How do I get in on a VR headset?

Sign in on your phone first, then pair the headset: either scan a code using Wallet connections in the Swop app, or open the pairing page on your phone and enter the code the headset shows. No password typing with a controller.

Do I need a seed phrase or crypto experience?

No. You need a swop.id, claimed in the Swop app. Swop is self-custodial — keys are generated and held on your device — but you sign in with your identity rather than by managing a seed phrase, and transactions are gas-sponsored, so you don’t need to hold SOL or ETH to transact.

Does a self-custodial wallet mean I legally own my virtual items?

Not automatically. In mainstream games virtual items are granted under a limited licence rather than sold as property, and publishers reserve the right to terminate accounts. Holding something in a wallet you control changes what can be done to your access — it can’t be silently frozen, and it isn’t confined to one platform’s trading system — but legal rights in virtual goods still depend on the terms you agreed to and your jurisdiction. Those are two different questions and we’re only claiming the first.

What does “beta” mean here?

That things will change under you. Improvements ship every week, so expect rough edges and expect features to move. Beta describes the state of the product; it isn’t a disclaimer that makes the rough edges yours.
