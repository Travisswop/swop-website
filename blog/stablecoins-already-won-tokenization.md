# Stablecoins are the tokenization that already won

Tokenization gets pitched as something coming for Treasuries, gold, and real estate. By the only metric that counts — value actually moved on-chain — it already happened, years ago, and the asset that won was the dollar.

A stablecoin is tokenization: a token issued against a reserve of dollars or dollar-equivalent assets held off-chain, redeemable against that reserve, and transferable on a public blockchain. Judged by that definition, stablecoins are not a preview of real-world-asset tokenization — they are its largest result to date. About $290 billion in stablecoins circulate on-chain today, against roughly $32–48 billion for every other tokenized real-world asset combined — Treasuries, gold, private credit, real estate. The asset everyone keeps describing as "about to be tokenized" already was, and it was cash.

That gap matters for anyone trying to read where tokenization is actually headed, because the industry's own numbers keep answering a different question than the one usually asked.

## What counts as tokenization

Strip away the branding and a tokenized asset is always the same shape: a claim and a redemption right on something that lives off-chain, represented by a token that moves on a public ledger instead of in a bank's or registrar's private database. Tokenized gold, like [PAX Gold](https://www.paxos.com/pax-gold), is a claim on physical gold held in a vault. A tokenized Treasury fund is a claim on a basket of government debt. A dollar-backed stablecoin is a claim on a reserve of dollars and short-term dollar instruments, redeemable 1:1.

None of that is a metaphor. It is the literal structure every tracker uses when it adds up "tokenized real-world assets," and under that structure, stablecoins are not kept in a separate bucket from Treasuries or gold — they are the biggest line item inside the same bucket.

## The numbers nobody states side by side

Figures differ by tracker and by week, because different trackers include different token sets and measure distributed value differently from represented value. But every major source — including [DefiLlama's live stablecoin tracker](https://defillama.com/stablecoins) — lands in the same place: stablecoins are not comparable in size to the rest of tokenized real-world assets — they dwarf them.

[CoinDesk Data's July 2026 report](https://data.coindesk.com/reports/stablecoins-tokenized-assets-report-july-2026) put total stablecoin market capitalization at roughly $308 billion, against $32.1 billion for non-stablecoin tokenized real-world assets — the highest level that second figure had ever reached. A separate Token Terminal breakdown from the same quarter put total on-chain tokenized value at about $346 billion, with stablecoins making up roughly 86% of it and the remainder — led by tokenized Treasuries at around $15 billion — split across every other category.

Non-stablecoin tokenization is not standing still. [CoinGecko's research desk](https://www.coingecko.com/learn/what-are-real-world-assets-exploring-rwa-protocols) recorded tokenized RWAs excluding stablecoins growing 256.7% over fifteen months, from $5.42 billion in January 2025 to $19.32 billion by the end of Q1 2026 — a real growth rate by any standard. It is also, on CoinGecko's own framing, still only about 6.4% the size of the stablecoin market, up from 2.7% a year earlier — fast growth off a small base. For a live view of how the categories currently split, [RWA.xyz's public tracker](https://app.rwa.xyz/) breaks out tokenized Treasuries, private credit, and commodities separately from stablecoins.

## Why stablecoins won first

The gap is not an accident of timing. Stablecoins had a structural head start that tokenized Treasuries and gold do not share.

Demand for dollars is already universal. A stablecoin does not need to create a market — it only needs to move an existing one onto a rail that settles faster and moves more easily across borders than a bank account does. We wrote about that rail property on its own terms in [Settlement is the product](https://www.swopme.co/blog/settlement-is-the-product): the thing tokenization changes first is not what the asset is, it’s how long it takes to actually have it.

Tokenized Treasuries and gold carry more friction before they can even compete. Most are gated to accredited or institutional buyers, and most route through a transfer-agent or allowlist layer that decides who is permitted to hold the token at all — the exact dynamic we covered in [Tokenized isn’t the same as yours](https://www.swopme.co/blog/tokenized-isnt-the-same-as-yours). A token that reverts if it lands in the wrong wallet is solving a compliance problem before it can solve a convenience problem. A dollar stablecoin, for the large majority of its users, has no such gate.

There’s also a simpler reason: most people who want gold or Treasury exposure can already get it through an existing brokerage account with a few taps. Tokenization has to beat an incumbent that is already easy to use. Cross-border dollar access is the opposite case — for a large share of the world, the existing option is genuinely bad, which is exactly the gap stablecoins stepped into first.

## What "winning" doesn’t mean

None of this means a stablecoin and a dollar are the same thing, and it’s worth being precise about where the analogy stops.

Holding a stablecoin means holding a claim on an issuer's promise to redeem it, backed by a reserve that issuer controls and reports on. That claim is only as good as the reserve, the audits behind it, and the issuer's own solvency — the identical counterparty question that applies to a tokenized Treasury fund or a tokenized gold bar. Tokenization moved the asset onto a faster, more transferable rail. It did not remove the issuer sitting in the middle of the claim, and it did not make every stablecoin interchangeable: different issuers carry different reserve composition, different audit cadence, and different jurisdictional backstops, which is the same "custody is the real variable" point this blog has made about tokenized RWAs generally.

This is a market-structure observation about tokenization broadly, not a claim about any stablecoin Swop issues — Swop doesn’t issue one.

## Where this goes next

If stablecoins are the proof of concept, the rest of tokenization is trying to repeat the trick for assets that don’t have a built-in global market waiting for them. McKinsey’s tokenization outlook — which, like the trackers above, counts tokenized financial assets excluding stablecoins and cryptocurrencies — put that market at roughly $2 trillion by 2030 in its base case, about $4 trillion in a bullish one, and nearer $1 trillion if adoption stays slow. Treat the range as directional rather than precise: it dates from 2024, and every forecaster defines the category differently, as the trackers above already show for today's numbers.

What that next wave borrows from stablecoins is the part that actually mattered: an asset you can hold yourself, move without asking permission, and settle instantly, rather than one that is merely described on a blockchain while the real custody arrangement stays exactly as it was. [USDC, USDC.e and pUSD](https://www.swopme.co/blog/usdc-vs-usdce) already show that even within "the tokenization that won," which specific token you’re holding still matters.

## FAQ

### Are stablecoins a form of real-world-asset tokenization?

Yes, by the same definition used for tokenized Treasuries or gold: a token issued against a reserve of a real-world asset held off-chain, redeemable against that reserve, and transferable on a public blockchain. A dollar-backed stablecoin is a token issued against a reserve of dollars or dollar-equivalents, redeemable 1:1. Trackers that measure tokenized real-world assets generally count stablecoins as the largest category of that market, not a separate one.

### Why have stablecoins grown so much faster than tokenized Treasuries or gold?

Demand for dollars is already universal, so a stablecoin doesn’t have to create a market, only move an existing one onto a faster rail. Tokenized Treasuries and gold have to do more work first: most are gated to accredited or institutional buyers, require a transfer-agent or allowlist layer to enforce who may hold the token, and are competing against an off-chain version of the same asset that most buyers can already access.

### Does holding a stablecoin mean you own a dollar?

No. It means you hold a claim on an issuer who promises to redeem your token for a dollar from its reserve. That claim is only as good as the issuer's reserve, its audits, and its own solvency — the same counterparty question that applies to tokenized Treasuries or gold. Tokenization moves the asset onto a faster, more transferable rail; it does not remove the issuer in the middle.
